Abstract 
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent the leading cause of mortality in Jordan and the broader 
MENA region, yet access to essential diagnostic tools like portable ECG devices remains critically 
limited due to high costs and lack of domestic production. This project addresses this healthcare 
and technological gap by designing a modular, scalable, low-cost, portable and web integrated  
ECG device tailored to the Jordanian healthcare environment. The device leverages a custom PCB 
for improved reliability and  cost-efficiency and incorporates Centralized data storage and web-
based connectivity  to facilitate ECG data reviewing and follow -up between patients and 
physicians. By designing locally, the project supports national goals of economic independence in 
medical technology and aims to reduce reliance on imported equipment.

Chapter 1: Introduction  
The electrocardiogram (ECG) is a powerful and indispensable tool in modern cardiology, playing 
a pivotal role in diagnosing and managing cardiovascular diseases, which remain the leading cause 
of mortality worldwide . According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular diseases 
account for an estimated 17.9 million deaths each year, underscoring the urgent need for early 
detection and intervention [1]. An ECG records the heart’s electrical activity over time, offering 
crucial insights into cardiac rhythm, conduction pathways, and overall structural integrity [2]. By 
detecting abnormalities such as arrhythmias, ischemic changes, and conduction defects, the ECG 
has saved countless lives by enabling timely medical intervention and guiding therapeutic 
strategies [3]. Its non-invasive nature and accessibility make it an essential component of both 
emergency and routine clinical settings, reinforcing its significance in reducing cardiovascular -
related morbidity and mortality [4]. 
Despite its clinical importance, access to ECG monitoring remains limited by the nature of existing 
systems. Conventional ECG devices are typically hospital-based, requiring specialized equipment, 
trained personnel, and controlled clinical environments. While high-end standalone and wearable 
devices have emerged in recent years, many of these solutions are either costly, limited in 
functionality, or designed primarily for short-term or single-lead monitoring, [5] as well as being 
limitedly available in Jordan . As a result, continuous and comprehensive cardiac monitoring 
remains inaccessible to a large portion of patients, particularly in low-resource settings. 
The need for accessible ECG monitoring is especially critical for patients requiring long -term 
observation outside hospital environments. Individuals with mild, moderate, or chronic cardiac 
conditions often benefit from extended monitoring to detect interm ittent or early -stage 
abnormalities that may not appear during brief clinical examinations. Home -based monitoring

solutions can reduce hospital burden, improve patient comfort, and enable earlier medical 
intervention. However, such systems must be affordable, easy to use, and capable of providing 
meaningful data to be truly effective. [6] 
In response to these challenges, this project presents the design and implementation of a low-cost, 
portable, and user-friendly multi-lead ECG monitoring system. The proposed system aims to 
bridge the gap between hospital -grade ECG devices and consumer -level solutions by offering 
reliable signal acquisition, wireless data transmission, and web -based visualization suitable for 
both patients and healthcare providers. The focus of this work is on affordability, accessibility, and 
practicality, making cardiac monitoring more widely available without compromising signal 
quality or clinical relevance.

electrophysiology and ECG fundamentals. Chapter 3 presents a review of related work and 
existing ECG monitoring systems. Chapter 4 details the system's methodology, including hardware 
design, signal acquisition, embedded firmware implementation, and software architecture. Chapter 
5 discusses system testing and validation of results. Finally, Chapter 6 concludes the dissertation 
and outlines potential future improvements. 
1.1 Anatomy and Physiology of the Heart 
The heart is a muscular pump whose structure and function are orchestrated through a symphony 
of electrical and mechanical processes [7]. Its structure includes four chambers, two atria and two 
ventricles—each with specialized roles in the process of blood circulation. The chambers are 
separated by robust fibrous tissues and valves that maintain unidirectional blood flow,  as seen in 
Figure 1.  
--- page 13 ---
3 
 
 
Figure 1. The gross anatomy of the heart [8]. 
The atria, ventricles, and valves are fundamental to cardiac function. The atria, with their thin 
walls, receive blood and initiate contraction via specialized conduction tissue . The  ventricles, 
particularly the robust left ventricle, generate the force needed to propel blood throughout the body. 
The heart valves, including the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) and semilunar (aortic and 
pulmonary) valves, ensure unidirectional blood flow by opening and closing in synchrony with the 
cardiac cycle, thereby preventing regurgitation and optimizing hemodynamics [7][9][10], ensuring 
efficient hemodynamics even under variable physiological demands [11][12]. 
At the microscopic level, the heart structure is equally complex. It comprises three distinct layers: 
the endocardium (inner lining), myocardium (thick muscular middle layer), and epicardium (outer 
layer) [9]. 
The electrical conduction system is the cornerstone of cardiac function. It is interplay between Its 
various components - the SA node, A V node, Bundle of His, bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers 
and Bachmann’s Bundle, seen in Figure 2, ensures that the heart beats with remarkable regularity 
and efficiency. 
 
 

--- page 14 ---
4 
 
The heart’s electrical activity represents a complex interplay of ionic exchanges and sig nal 
propagation culminating in mechanical contraction. Electrical signals in the heart directly 
correspond to the phases of depolarization and repolarization. When an action potential is 
generated in a cardiomyocyte, depolarization occurs first, initiated by the rapid influx of sodium 
ions (Na⁺). This sudden positive shift in membrane potential triggers the opening of voltage-gated 
calcium channels, allowing calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to enter the cell. This influx of Ca²⁺ not only 
sustains the plateau phase of th e action potential but also plays a crucial role in excitation –
contraction coupling, where it stimulates the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release additional Ca²⁺, 
ultimately leading to myocardial contraction [13][14]. 
Repolarization follows as potassium ions (K⁺) exit the cell, restoring the resting membrane 
potential and allowing the cardiomyocyte to prepare for the next action potential. This 
repolarization phase ensures that the electrical cycle resets properly, prev enting sustained 
depolarization and allowing coordinated contractions [10]. 
This entire process is regulated by the autonomic nervous system, hormones, and local paracrine 
factors, allowing the heart to adapt swiftly to changing metabolic demands [7]. 
 
Figure 2. The electrical conduction system of the heart [11].

Oxygen plays a crucial role in cardiac excitat ion–contraction coupling, which is the process that 
links the electrical signals in the heart to its physical contraction [15]. This happens by enabling 
the production of ATP (adenosine triphosphate), the energy currency of cells, through a process 
called oxidative phosphorylation, which takes place inside the mitochondria. ATP is vital because 
it powers the work of the ion pumps, including the sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA), 
which helps store calcium inside a compartment of the cell. Another is the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which 
keeps the balance of sodium and potassium  ions in and out of the cell, creating the electrical 
environment needed for signals to move across heart cells [16]. 
When the body does not get enough oxygen, a condition known as hypoxia, less ATP is made. This 
weakens the activity of pumps like SERCA, causing too much calcium to stay in the fluid part of 
the cell instead of being stored [15][16]. As a result, the heart’s action potential can last too long, 
the reset period between beats (the refractory period) may change, and dangerous irregular 
arrhythmia can occur [17]. Low oxygen also causes harmful molecules known as reactive oxygen 
species (ROS) to build up. These molecules can damage proteins and interfere with ion channels, 
which are gateways that control how ions flow in and out of the cell, affecting both the hea rtbeat 
and the strength of heart muscle contractions (myocardial contractility) [17].  
Initiation of the heartbeat begins at the sinoatrial (SA) node, the heart’s natural pacemaker located 
in the right atrium near the entrance of the superior vena cava. Histologically, the SA node 
comprises specialized cardiomyocytes with an intrinsic abilit y to depolarize spontaneously, a 
property enabled by a unique mix of ion channels  [18][19]. The SA node generates electrical 
impulse at atrial depolarization. Its spontaneous depolarization is regulated by autonomic input , 
which is sympathetic stimulation that accelerates the heart rate, while parasympathetic stimulation 
decelerates it. 
--- page 16 ---
6 
 
Following the SA node, the impulse reaches the atrioventricular (A V) node, positioned at the 
junction of the atria and ventricles. The A V node serves as a critical relay station that introduces a 
physiological delay of approximately 0.1 seconds, allowing f or complete atrial contraction and 
optimal ventricular filling  [10][19]. Histologically more compact than the SA node and rich in 
connexins (gap junction proteins), the A V node facilitates a slow yet coordinated propagation of 
electrical impulses. This delay is not only mechanically efficient but also protective, preventing 
excessively rapid atrial impulses from overloading the ventricles [11]. 
After the A V node, the electrical impulse travels through the Bundle of His, a slender fibrous 
structure that represents the only electrical connection between the atria and ventricles. The Bundle 
of His then branches into the right and left bundle branches, each rapidly transmitting the impulse 
to their respective ventricles [20]. These fiber tracts, insulated by connective tissue, ensure that the 
electrical impulse is delivered with speed and efficiency [13][18]. 
At the terminal ends of the conduction network lie the Purkinje fibers, which are large, fast -
conducting cells responsible for rapidly spreading the electrical impulse throughout the ventricular 
myocardium. Their extensive branching and high conduction speed ensure that the ventricles 
contract almost synchronously, which is essential for optimal blood ejection during systole. 
Purkinje fibers posse ss a unique cellular structure characterized by fewer myofibrils and a high 
density of mitochondria, making them specialized for rapid impulse propagation rather than force 
generation [13][21]. 
As for the Bachmann’s bundle, it is a crucial interatrial conduction pathway that facilitates the 
rapid transmission of electrical impulses from the right atrium to the left atrium, ensuring that both 
chambers contract in a coordinated fashion. This synchronization is vital for effective  atrial 
emptying and optimal ventricular filling, which ultimately enhances the efficiency of the cardiac 
--- page 17 ---
7 
 
cycle. Its function is integral to maintaining normal atrial rhythm, and disturbances in this pathway 
have been linked to the development of atrial arrhythmia [10][19]. 
At the molecular level, the heart’s efficiency is further augmented by the precise regulation of ion 
channels, gap junctions, and intracellular signaling pathways. The connectivity provided by gap 
junctions allows for the rapid propagation of electrical im pulses between adjacent myocytes, 
ensuring that contraction occurs in a unified and synchronized [19][21]. 
1.2 Pathology of the Heart 
The pathology of the heart represents a variety of  clinical conditions that manifest distinct 
electrocardiographic features, each reflecting specific underlying structural and functional 
abnormalities. At its core, cardiac pathology encompasses a wide array of disorders ranging from 
ischemic injuries and myocardial infarctions to various conduction disturbances and arrhythmia, 
each altering the balance of electrical and mechanical activity.  The ECG waveform consists of 
several distinct components—the P wave, QRS complex, T wave, and sometimes a U wave—each 
corresponding to specific electrical and mechanical events in the heart, as seen in Figure 3. These 
alterations are often revealed through detailed analysis of the ECG, where deviations in the PQRST 
complex offer clues to the nature and extent of the disease process [9].  
 
Figure 3. A PQRST wave. 
 

--- page 18 ---
8 
 
The P wave represents atrial depolarization, indicating the initiation of the heartbeat at the SA node 
and subsequent atrial contraction. It is usually small and upright in most leads, with abnormalities 
signifying atrial enlargement or conduction delays [18]. 
The QRS complex corresponds to ventricular depolarization, marking the most critical phase of 
electrical activation. This complex includes the Q wave (initial downward deflection), the R wave 
(large upward deflection), and the S wave (subsequent downward d eflection). The width and 
morphology of the QRS complex provides insight into conduction disorders such as bundle branch 
blocks or ventricular hypertrophy [7]. 
Following ventricular contraction, the T wave reflects ventricular repolarization, a crucial phase 
where the heart resets for the next beat. Flattened or inverted T waves may indicate ischemia, 
electrolyte imbalances, or ventricular strain [22]. 
In some cases, a U wave appears after the T wave, which is often linked to hypokalemia or 
abnormal repolarization processes [21]. 
Fortunately, the use of an ECG paved a path for detecting many dangerous, yet common, 
conditions.  
a. Myocardial Infarction  
Myocardial infarction (MI), commonly known as a heart attack, is  perhaps the most extensively 
studied cardiac pathology, characterized by the abrupt occlusion of coronary arteries leading to 
ischemia and subsequent necrosis of the affected myocardial tissue. The ischemic cascade 
produces specific ECG changes, such as ST-segment elevation or depression, pathological Q  
Waves, and T -wave inversions, each correlating with the infarct size and location  [23]. In 
particular, ST-segment elevation, when persistent as seen in Figure 4 is a hallmark for diagnosing

Cardiac arrhythmias are a variety of critical  pathologies that have a direct  effect on the ECG

by an irregular rhythm and the absence of distinct P waves, reflecting the chaotic atrial electrical

This condition is often linked to atrial enlargement, fibrosis, or systemic conditions that predispose

c. Ventricular Tachycardia & Fibrillation 
Ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation are life-threatening disturbances of ventricular 
rhythm that lead to rapid and unsynchronized contraction, dramatically altering the QRS complex 
morphology and, in the case of fibrillation, obliterating any discernible pattern  [18]. Ventricular

d. Conduction abnormalities - Right and left bundle branch blocks 
Conduction abnormalities further complicate the electrical narrative of the heart. A bundle branch 
block, whether right or left, results in delayed depolarization of one ventricle, thereby widening 
the QRS complex and often producing secondary ST -T changes . Right bundle branch block 
(RBBB) occurs due to impaired conduction in the right bundle branch, leading to sequential rather 
than simultaneous activation of the ventricles. This manifests in lead V1 as a characteristic RSR’ 
pattern, commonly described as "rabbit ears," where the initial small R wave represents normal 
septal depolarization, the deep S wave reflects dominant left ventricular activation, and the 
prominent second R wave (R'), indicates delayed right ventricular depolarization, as shown in

Left bundle branch block (LBBB), in contrast, disrupts conduction through the left bundle branch,

Both RBBB and LBBB can be benign or indicative of underlying pathology such as ischemic heart 
disease, cardiomyopathy, or structural heart disorders, so further clinical correlation and evaluation 
is needed.

e. Conduction abnormalities - atrioventricular (A V) block 
A V block, categorized into first, second, and third degrees, manifests as prolonged PR intervals or 
intermittent non-conducted atrial impulses, leading up to a complete dissociation between atrial 
and ventricular activity in third -degree block. Each of these conduction defects imparts distinct 
ECG signatures, as seen in Figure 10, that not only aid in diagnosis but also provide prognostic 
information regarding the underlying structural integrity of the conduction system [13]. 
 
f. Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathies  
Cardiomyopathies, encompassing both hypertrophic and dilated  forms, represent intrinsic 
disorders of the myocardium that often result in aberrant electrical conduction and mechanical 
dysfunction [9]. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, as shown in Figure 11 , asymmetric septal  
thickening and myocardial disarray can lead to abnormal repolarization patterns, including deep, 
inverted T waves and potential ST-segment abnormalities [20][28].

Chapter 2: Theoretical Background 
The ECG machine functions by detecting electrical signals produced by cardiac muscle 
contractions, amplifying them, filtering out noise, and converting them into a readable waveform. 
The resulting tracings allow clinicians to assess heart conditions such a s arrhythmias, ischemia, 
and infarctions [35]. 
2.1 The Historical Evolution of ECG Machines 
The development of ECG technology spans over a century, evolving from large and heavy early 
devices to highly sophisticated digital systems. The first practical ECG machine was introduced in 
--- page 31 ---
21 
 
the early 1900s by Dutch physiologist Willem Einthoven, who developed the string galvanometer, 
a large, complex device capable of recording heart activity [36]. Despite its size and operational 
challenges, this invention laid the groundwork for modern electrocardiography. 
By the mid-20th century, advancements in electronics enabled the transition from vacuum tube -
based ECG machines to transistorized devices, significantly reducing their size and improving 
portability [37]. In 1957, Norman J. Holter pioneered ambulatory ECG monitoring, introducing 
the Holter monitor, which allowed continuous cardiac monitoring over extended periods. This 
breakthrough was instrumental in diagnosing intermittent arrhythmias [38][39]. 
The digital revolution in the 1980s and 1990s further transformed ECG technology, introducing 
computerized interpretation algorithms that enhanced diagnostic accuracy. Modern ECG machines 
integrate AI-driven analytics, wireless transmission, and cloud -based storage, facilitating remote 
monitoring and large -scale data analysis [40]. These innovations have expanded the role of an 
ECG in preventive cardiology, allowing for early detection of cardiac abnormalities [41]. 
2.2 Components of a Standard ECG 
An ECG machine consists of several key components, each playing a vital role in signal 
acquisition, processing, and interpretation.  
2.2.1 Electrodes  
The electrodes are the first point of contact, placed on the skin to detect bioelectrical signals. These 
electrodes, usually made of silver/silver chloride due to its stable electrode -electrolyte interface, 
which minimizes polarization effects and enhances signal fidelity  [42], they work by capturing 
potential differences generated by the depolarization and repolarization of cardiac muscle cells 
[43]. Electrodes are connected to the machine via leads, which provide different perspectives of 
cardiac electrical activity. The electrodes interface with the body through an electrolytic gel that 
--- page 32 ---
22 
 
reduces contact impedance and improves signal quality [44]. A 12-lead ECG system provides a 
comprehensive view of cardiac electrical activity by acquiring signals from different anatomical 
perspectives. A standard 12 -lead ECG records electrical signals from 10 electrode placements, 
creating a comprehensive representation of heart function [38]. It consists of three bipolar limb 
leads (I, II, III), three augmented unipolar leads (a VR, aVL, aVF), and six precordial leads (V1 -
V6). These leads are placed at standardized positions on the body to obtain signals from multiple 
orientations of the heart’s electrical activity [45]. 
In addition to the standard 12-lead system, ECGs can be recorded using fewer leads depending on 
clinical requirements. Single-lead ECGs are systems that use only one pair of electrodes to measure 
cardiac activity, commonly used in wearable monitors and personal ECG devices for rhythm 
monitoring rather than full cardiac diagnosis  [46]. Three-lead ECGs are typically used for basic 
cardiac monitoring in hospital settings ; three-lead ECGs record leads I, II, and III, providing a 
limited but essential view of cardiac rhythm and rate. They are widely used in ambulatory 
monitoring and emergency settings [47]. Five-lead ECGs are frequently used in intensive care and 
telemetry units; five-lead systems provide continuous cardiac monitoring, adding a precordial lead 
(V1 or V5) for additional waveform detail [48]. Six-lead ECGs are a n advancement seen in 
portable devices; six-lead ECGs add more precordial lead perspectives to enhance rhythm analysis 
while maintaining a compact form factor [49].

electrical activity is relatively weak (typically 0.5 –5 mv). The amplifier enhances these signals

2.7.1 AD8232 Instrumentation Amplifier 
After the ECG signal is collected from the electrodes and safely limited using series protection 
resistors, it is fed into the instrumentation amplifier stage of the AD8232. The purpose of this stage 
is to amplify the very small differential ECG signal whi le rejecting unwanted signals that appear 
equally on both electrodes, such as power -line interference and environmental noise. This makes 
the IA a critical stage in ensuring that the recorded ECG waveform is accurate and suitable for 
further processing. 
Internally, the instrumentation amplifier shown in Figure 23 is composed of two closely matched 
transconductance amplifiers (GM1 and GM2), a dc -blocking amplifier (HPA), and an integrator 
formed by capacitor C1 and an operational amplifier. The first transconductance amplifier, GM1, 
senses the voltage difference be tween the two input electrodes and converts this differential 
voltage into a proportional current. Through the internal feedback network, the circuit forces the 
same voltage to appear across the inputs of the second transconductance amplifier, GM2. When 
both amplifiers are perfectly balanced, the generated currents match. Any small mismatch between 
these currents produces an error current, which is then integrated across capacitor C1. This 
integration process generates a voltage at the output of the instrum entation amplifier (IAOUT), 
resulting in a stable and amplified version of the original ECG signal. 
--- page 42 ---

The overall gain of the instrumentation amplifier is internally set to 100 using a resistive feedback 
network that feeds a scaled version of the output signal back through GM2. At the same time, the 
dc-blocking amplifier continuously integrates slow changes and deviations from the reference 
voltage. 
This design allows the instrumentation amplifier to handle large dc offsets at electrode inputs (up 
to ±300 mV) without saturating. These offsets are effectively shifted to the GM2 inputs with equal 
magnitude and opposite polarity, ensuring that the small-amplitude ECG signal of interest remains 
within the linear operating range of the amplifier. 
To further improve performance in real-world conditions, the AD8232 includes an internal charge 
pump that increases the effective supply voltage available to the transconductance amplifiers. This 
additional headroom expands the common -mode voltage range of  the instrumentation amplifier 
and reduces the risk of saturation in the presence of large common -mode signals, such as 50/60 
Hz mains interference. The charge pump operates using an internal oscillator with a frequency of 
approximately 500 kHz, which is w ell above the ECG frequency band and therefore does not 
interfere with the recorded signal.

2.7.2 AD8232 Operational Amplifiers as Active Filters 
Following the instrumentation amplifier stage, the ECG signal is passed through a high-pass filter 
(HPF) to remove unwanted low -frequency components and residual dc offsets  caused by slow 
motion artifacts and respiration, and baseline wander caused by changes in electrode –skin 
impedance. Although the instrumentation amplifier can tolerate large dc electrode offsets without 
saturation, additional filtering is required to stabilize the ECG baseline and improve signal clarity. 
To achieve this offset rejection,  the AD8232 implements an RC network connected between the 
instrumentation amplifier output (IAOUT), HPSENSE, and HPDRIVE pins, forming a dc -
blocking high-pass filter stage.  The AD8232 also supports the impleme ntation of extending the 
high-pass filter using a dc -blocking topology to form a second pole for improved low -frequency 
rejection. 
After baseline stabilization through high-pass filtering, the signal is subjected to low-pass filtering 
to suppress unwanted high -frequency components arising from electromyographic noise, 
electromagnetic sources, and switching activity from nearby electronic circuits. If not attenuated, 
these components can obscure clinically relevant waveform features and degrade signal 
interpretability. Low-pass filtering is implemented  in the AD8232 using an internal operational 
amplifier in conjunction with external RC components, forming an active low -pass filter stage. 
This filtering limits the upper bandwidth of the signal, ensuring that the preserved frequency 
content corresponds to the physiological ECG range while reducing noise prior to analog-to-digital 
conversion. The AD8232 supports a two or three -pole low -pass filter as well, with cutoff 
frequencies that can be externally configured via resistor -capacitor (RC) networks. These filters 
are crucial for isolating the ECG signal from noise components like baseline wander and muscle 
noise.

In both the high -pass and low -pass filtering stages, the AD8232 reference output (REFOUT) is 
used to establish a stable mid-supply DC operating point for the ECG signal. In the high-pass filter,

Referencing either filter stage to ground instead of REFOUT would shift the signal bias, drive the

2.7.3 Right Leg Drive (RLD) 
The right leg drive circuitry is an essential component in ECG acquisition systems and is primarily 
used to improve common -mode noise rejection, particularly against power -line interference. In 
practical environments, the human body behaves like an antenna and can pick up electromagnetic 
interference from surrounding electrical sources. This interference appears as a common -mode 
voltage at the ECG electrode inputs and can significantly degrade signal quality if not properly 
suppressed. 
--- page 46 ---
36 
 
The RLD amplifier  operates by sensing the common -mode voltage present at the inputs of the 
instrumentation amplifier and actively feeding back an inverted version of this signal into the 
subject through the driven electrode, typically connected to the right leg. By injectin g this 
compensating signal, the RLD circuit counteracts common -mode voltage variations at the 
electrode–skin interface, thereby reducing the amplitude of interference seen at the differential 
inputs and improving the overall common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) of the ECG system. 
 
In the AD8232, the common-mode signal is derived internally from the transconductance amplifier 
GM1 and routed to the inverting input of the internal RLD amplifier (A2) through an internal 150 
kΩ resistor, as shown in Figure 25.  
The non -inverting input of the RLD amplifier is biased to the reference voltage (REFOUT), 
ensuring stable operation in a single -supply configuration. This internal architecture allows the 
RLD loop to function as an active feedback system that stabilizes the patient’s common -mode 
potential relative to the analog front end. 
Proper operation of the RLD circuit relies on the availability of a stable reference voltage rather 
than true ground. In single -supply ECG systems, using 0 V ground as the reference would cause

the device are centered around this reference voltage, ensuring proper handling of bipolar ECG

the allowable common-mode range of the instrumentation amplifier. 
If an electrode becomes disconnected, the affected input is no longer constrained by the subject’s 
potential and drifts toward the positive supply rail due to the pull -up resistor. When the input 
voltage approaches within approximately 0.5 V of the positiv e supply, the AD8232 detects a dc 
lead-off condition. Because each input is monitored independently, dc lead -off detection allows 
identification of which specific electrode has been disconnected. 
Lead-off status in dc mode is reported through two digital comparator outputs. The LOD+ pin is 
driven high when the electrode connected to the non-inverting input (+IN) is disconnected, while 
the LOD− pin is driven high when the electrode connected to the inverting input (−IN) is 
disconnected. DC lead-off detection mode is selected by driving the AC/DC control pin low. 
AC lead-off detection is designed for two -electrode ECG configurations and does not require a 
driven right-leg electrode. In this mode, the AD8232 injects a small high -frequency current into 
the input terminals and detects changes in the resulting differential voltage caused by variations in 
electrode impedance. If an electrode becomes disconnected, the increased impedance triggers a 
lead-off condition. 
--- page 50 ---
40 
 
Unlike dc lead -off detection, ac lead -off detection cannot distinguish which electrode has been 
disconnected. In this mode, the LOD+ output is driven high during a lead -off event, while LOD− 
remains low. AC lead-off detection is selected by driving the AC/DC control pin high. [86] 
 
2.8 Microcontroller (ESP32) 
The ESP32, shown in Figure 27, was used as the microcontroller unit in this project. It  is a low-
cost, low-power system-on-chip (SoC) microcontroller developed by Espressif Systems. It features 
a dual-core processor with integrated Wi-Fi and Bluetooth capabilities, making it ideal for wireless 
communication and Internet of Things applications.  
The ESP32 operates at a core voltage of 3.3V and supports a wide input voltage range via external 
regulators. It includes a variety of peripheral interfaces such as Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI), 
Inter-Integrated Circuit ( I²C), Universal Asynchronous Receiver -Transmitter (UART), Pulse -
Width Modulation (PWM), and a 12-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) that can digitize analog 
input signals at rates suitable for real-time biomedical monitoring [87].

(SCL) and a serial data line (SDA). The clock line synchronizes data transfer, while the data line

2.9 ADS1115 Analog-to-Digital Converter 
The ADS1115 is a precision, low-power, 16-bit ADC designed for accurate measurement of low-
frequency, low-amplitude signals. It employs a delta -sigma (ΔΣ) conversion architecture, where 
the input signal is oversampled and processed through a noise -shaping modulator, followed by a 
digital low -pass filter and decimation stage. This process shifts quantization noise to higher 
frequencies and suppresses it digitally, resulting in improved effective resolution within the signal 
band of interest.  
A key feature of the ADS1115 is its support for differential input measurements. Differential 
conversion allows the ADC to measure the voltage difference between two input terminals while 
rejecting common-mode voltages that appear equally on both inputs. 
To support low-power and portable systems, the ADS1115 includes an internal voltage reference 
and multiple operating modes, including single -shot and continuous conversion. Communication 
with a host microcontroller is achieved through an I²C interface, whi ch provides a compact and 
efficient means of configuring the converter and retrieving digitized samples. [89]

2.10 Web-Based System Architecture and Communication Technologies 
Modern biomedical monitoring systems increasingly rely on web -based architectures to enable 
remote access, real-time visualization, and scalable data management. Such systems are commonly 
structured according to a client–server model, where system functionality is divided between a 
backend server and one or more frontend clients. This architectural separation improves 
modularity, security, and maintainability, particularly in systems that continuously acquire 
physiological signals such as ECGs [90]. 
The backend forms the central control layer of the system. It is responsible for receiving 
physiological data from acquisition devices, managing system state, performing signal handling 
and processing, enforcing access control, and storing data for later retrieval. I n continuous 
monitoring applications, the backend must be capable of handling time-stamped data streams while 
organizing them into well-defined recording sessions. This approach allows data to be validated, 
processed, and archived in a controlle d and reproducible manner before being made available to 
user interfaces [91]. 
Interaction between system components is typically achieved through RESTful web services, 
which are standard web -based communication methods  that allow system components to 
communicate by sending and receiving data through simple web requests based on widely used 
internet standards including HTTPs. In REST -based systems, system resources such as sessions, 
users, and recorded data are accessed t hrough clearly defined specific server addresses designed 
to perform particular actions, called endpoints, using simple request–response interactions [92]. 
While RESTful communication is effective for control operations and data retrieval, real -time 
biomedical signal visualization requires continuous, data transfer with little delay. For this purpose, 
many modern systems employ WebSocket-based communication, which establishes a persistent, 
--- page 53 ---
43 
 
bidirectional connection between the backend and connected user interfaces (such as web 
browsers). It enables near-real-time streaming of physiological signals and system events, allowing 
ECG waveforms and alerts to be delivered to user interfaces with minimal delay [93]. 
The front end represents the client-side component of the system and is typically implemented as 
a browser-based interface. Its primary role is to present physiological data in an interpretable form, 
provide controls for session management, and allow users to review re cordings. By performing 
data processing and storage on the backend server, the frontend remains focused on displaying 
information and interacting with the user. [90], [91].

4.1 Introduction to Methodology 
This section presents the methodology followed in the design and implementation of the portable 
ECG monitoring system. The adopted methodology follows a structured engineering approach 
encompassing hardware design of the Analog Front end, digitization, transmission and embedded 
system integration, backend software development, and web-based frontend implementation. 
The system was developed as a set of independent modules that together implement the complete 
ECG monitoring workflow, from signal acquisition to browser -based visualization. Each 
subsystem was designed and implemented separately and then integrated to ens ure reliable 
operation across the full acquisition and monitoring pipeline. 
The following subsections describe the hardware architecture, embedded firmware, backend 
processing system, frontend interface, and validation methodology employed in this project. 
4.2 System Overview  
The ECG monitoring system implements a sequential acquisition and data -handling pipeline that 
spans analog signal conditioning, digital transmission, backend processing, and web -based 
visualization, as illustrated in the system block diagram in Figure 28. 
At the hardware level, ECG biopotential signals are acquired using surface electrodes arranged in 
three limb-lead configurations (RA–LA, RA–LL, and LA–LL), each referenced to a driven right-
leg (RLD) electrode. Each electrode pair is connected to a dedicat ed AD8232 analog front -end 
module, which outputs a conditioned analog ECG waveform. All analog front -end modules are 
powered from a common 3.3 V supply.

the recommended safety limit of 10 µA, even under fault conditions. At the same time, the chosen 
value allows the RLD loop to operate ef fectively without significantly degrading common -mode 
feedback performance.

4.3.8 Power Supply and Decoupling 
Reliable ECG acquisition requires a clean and stable power supply, as excessive noise on the 
supply pins can directly couple into the sensitive analog front -end circuitry and degrade signal 
quality. The AD8232, like other precision analog devices, is therefore decoupled using local bypass 
capacitors to suppress supply noise across a wide frequency range. 
In this design, the AD8232 is powered from a 3.3 V supply, and a 0.1 µF capacitor is placed as 
close as possible to the supply pin , as seen in Figure 35. This capacitor provides low impedance 
decoupling at high frequencies and attenuates fast transients and switching noise that may originate 
from digital circuitry or external power sources. Close placement is critical, as long trace lengths 
can introduce parasitic inductance that reduces the effectiveness of high-frequency decoupling. 
While adequate decoupling is essential for stable operation, excessive decoupling capacitance can 
increase inrush current and power dissipation during power cycling. Therefore, the selected 
capacitor values represent a balance between noise suppression and  power efficiency. Together,

4.4 ADC Interface and Signal Conditioning 
After analog conditioning by the instrumentation amplifier, high -pass filter, and low -pass filter 
stages, the ECG signal is routed directly to the analog-to-digital converter for sampling and digital 
processing. In this design, the output of each AD8232 low-pass filter stage is connected directly to 
the AIN0, AIN1, and AIN2 inputs of the ADS1115 ADC. 
The AD8232 analog front-end is designed to interface directly with data acquisition systems and 
provides an output stage suitable for driving ADC inputs without the need for an additional buffer 
amplifier. While ADC inputs may draw transient currents during sampling, the ECG signal in this 
design is bandwidth -limited and sourced from an actively filtered output stage, allowing stable 
operation when interfaced directly with the ADS1115. 
The voltage presented to the ADC input corresponds to the filtered ECG signal biased around the 
internal reference voltage generated by the AD8232. This biasing ensures that the signal remains 
within the allowable input range of the ADS1115 while preserving ECG waveform integrity. The 
direct ADC interface simplifies the signal path, reduces component count, and enables reliable 
digitization of the conditioned ECG signal.

ECG data ingestion in the proposed system is initiated only after an explicit recording session has 
been started by the patient through the web interface. This design enforces a controlled acquisition 
workflow in which all incoming ECG samples are associat ed with a known patient and a clearly 
defined recording interval. This approach improves system reliability and protects data integrity 
during network interruptions or user-side errors. 
Once a session is active, the ESP32 transmits digitized ECG samples to the backend server using 
HTTP POST requests. Data are sent in batch form, where each request contains a short sequence 
of consecutive samples along with their corresponding timestamps. Batch-based transmission was 
selected to reduce network overhead while maintaining sufficient temporal resolution for real-time 
monitoring. 
Upon reception, the backend associates incoming ECG samples with a valid active recording 
session, ensuring that all acquired data are correctly organized within a defined acquisition interval 
and preventing loss or misassignment during continuous monitoring.

4.7.2 Data Filtering and Processing  
Each ECG channel is sampled at 250 Hz on the hardware, timestamped, and transmitted to the 
backend in short batches. Prior to storage and visualization, the digitized signals are digitally 
filtered using a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.5 Hz to suppress baseline wander and 
a low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 40 Hz to attenuate high-frequency noise. This passband 
preserves the primary morphological features of the ECG waveform required for visualization and 
heart-rate estimation. Following filtering, the standard limb leads (Lead I, Lead II, Lead III) and 
augmented leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) are derived mathematically from the filtered signals using 
standard Einthoven and Goldberger relationships. 
Processed samples are not written on the database immediately on arrival. Instead, samples are 
temporarily stored in an in-memory buffer and periodically committed to the database in grouped 
transactions. This buffering strategy reduces disk write overhead and ensures stable system 
performance during continuous high-rate data acquisition. 
4.7.3 Heart Rate Extraction and Processing 
Heart rate estimation was implemented on the backend server using a real -time, Pan–Tompkins–
inspired signal processing algorithm applied to the derived Lead II ECG signal. Incoming ECG 
samples are processed sequentially, where the signal first undergoes di fferentiation to emphasize 
rapid slope changes associated with QRS complexes, followed by squaring to enhance peak 
prominence. A moving window integration stage of approximately 150 ms is then applied to 
capture the energy of the QRS complex while suppressing high-frequency noise. 
An adaptive thresholding mechanism is used to identify candidate R-peaks based on the integrated 
signal amplitude. To prevent false detections, a refractory period of 350 ms is enforced between 
--- page 92 ---
82 
 
successive detections, and physiologically implausible RR intervals are rejected. Detected RR 
intervals are stored in a rolling buffer, and heart rate is computed using the median RR value to 
reduce sensitivity to outliers and transient artifacts. 
To improve display stability, heart rate updates are rate -limited such that changes occur only at 
fixed time intervals and are constrained to gradual transitions. The resulting heart rate values are 
stored alongside ECG samples and made available for real -time visualization and post -session 
analysis. 
4.7.4 Authentication  
To protect sensitive patient data and ensure controlled system operation, the backend enforces 
authentication and role-based access control. User roles, including patient and clinician accounts, 
are managed by backend logic, restricting access to system fu nctions such as session initiation, 
historical data retrieval, and alert review. This design ensures that ECG data and session controls 
are accessible only to authorized users, which is particularly important in medical monitoring 
applications. 
4.7.5 Session Termination, Data Storage, and Report Generation 
Recording sessions are explicitly terminated by the user through the web interface, signaling the 
end of ECG acquisition. Upon session termination, the backend finalizes data handling by closing 
the active recording interval and preventing any further samp les from being associated with the 
session. This controlled termination ensures that each ECG recording represents a well -defined 
and complete acquisition period. 
All ECG samples are associated with timestamps to preserve temporal accuracy across acquisition, 
processing, and storage. This temporal organization ensures that ECG waveforms, derived

persistently and organized under the corresponding patient and session identifiers. This structure

to a specific pati ent and acquisition event, which is essential for post -recording review and

Following session termination, the backend automatically generates a structured ECG session

4.10 Optimization Considerations 
In the implemented analog front -end design, using relatively large resistor values allows for the 
use of smaller capacitors. Filter cutoff frequencies and gain settings were selected to balance noise 
suppression and signal fidelity while minimizing external component count. These approaches 
reduce overall component footprint and support compact PCB layout without compromising filter 
performance in the ECG bandwidth. 
The use of the ESP32 microcontroller consolidates multiple system functions into a single device, 
including digital processing, peripheral interfacing, and wireless communication. By integrating 
these subsystems directly on the microcontroller, the need for external communication modules 
and associated supporting circuitry is eliminated. This reduces PCB area, component count, power 
routing complexity, and potential points of failure. 
The system was optimized for single -supply operations across both analog and digital domains. 
The AD8232 analog front -end operates from the same low -voltage supply as the ESP32, 
eliminating the need for dual -rail power generation or level -shifting circuitry. Biasing the ECG 
signal around an internal mid -supply reference enables safe and stable signal acquisition while 
minimizing power consumption and simplifying power management. 
--- page 102 ---
92 
 
To improve digitization accuracy without increasing system complexity, an external high -
resolution ADC (ADS1115) was used instead of relying on the ESP32’s internal ADC. This choice 
enables precise capture of low -amplitude ECG signals while allowing the ES P32 to focus on 
communication and control tasks. Separating signal acquisition and wireless transmission 
improves overall system efficiency and scalability. 
The architecture was optimized for scalability by using identical analog front -end channels and a 
shared digital acquisition and transmission pipeline. This modular approach allows additional ECG 
channels to be added with minimal changes to firmware and ba ckend infrastructure, supporting 
future expansion toward higher-lead configurations without redesigning the core system.

Variations in electrode pin assignment resulted in observable differences in amplitude scaling, 
signal polarity, and baseline characteristics. Although all three front-end modules were driven by

differences in electrode mapping and internal signal conditioning behavior. The AD8232 is not a 
simple subtractor; electrode configuration directly influences common -mode rejection, filter 
response, and reference stabilization.

physiological limb leads in the clinical sense. Instead, they represent distinct conditioned 
projections of the same ECG signal processed through separate analog front -end pathways under 
different electrode configurations. While this experimental setup does not enable true Einthoven 
triangle reconstruction or clinical multi-lead ECG acquisition, it is sufficient for validating analog

Chapter 7: Future Work 
Although the current system successfully demonstrates multi -lead ECG acquisition, wireless 
streaming, visualization, and local data archiving, several enhancements can elevate it toward a 
more clinically presentable and product-oriented platform.  
In future iterations, the ESP32 will be limited to signal acquisition, timestamping, and wireless 
transmission, while advanced signal processing and diagnostic computation will be offloaded to a 
dedicated edge processor such as a Raspberry Pi. This upgrade  would enable more robust digital 
filtering, reliable QRS/R -peak detection, and stable heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV) 
computation, improving timing accuracy, buffering, and overall system reliability. 
Building on this processing capability, future work can introduce an AI -assisted diagnostic layer 
to support detection of common rhythm abnormalities and clinically relevant signal patterns. 
Machine learning or validated algorithmic libraries can be deploy ed on the Raspberry Pi to 
generate decision -support indicators, which would be displayed through an existing dedicated 
diagnostic section of the CardioVault web interface.  
To enhance portability and scalability, future versions can incorporate secure cloud connectivity 
for remote monitoring and long-term storage using encrypted communication protocols, alongside 
local buffering to ensure reliability during network interruptions. 
--- page 119 ---
109 
 
A dedicated power management subsystem incorporating battery operation, an integrated charging 
and protection circuit, and seamless switching between battery power and externally supplied DC 
power would further enhance system robustness and support extende d continuous operation. 
Additionally, a compact, electrically insulating (non-conductive) enclosure could be employed to 
protect internal circuitry, prevent accidental electrical contact, and further enhance user safety. 
Although the hardware layout includes support for lead-off detection through the AD8232 analog 
front-end, this feature was not fully utilized in the current system implementation. The lead -off 
detection output was not connected to the ESP32 microcontroller, and corresponding firmware and 
backend handling were therefore not implemented. 
Future work could involve routing the lead -off detection signal to a dedicated ESP32 GPIO and 
integrating firmware logic to monitor electrode connection status in real time. This would enable 
automatic detection of disconnected or poorly attached electrodes and allow the system to generate 
user alerts through the web interface. Incorporating lead -off status into recorded sessions would 
further enhance data reliability and improve user feedback during home-based ECG acquisition. 
Finally, expanded testing and validation, including Clinical Level ECG simulator, testing under 
unstable or congested network, longer continuous sessions, and human trials under ethical 
approval, would be essential steps toward improved clinical credibility and potential certification 
readiness.

Experimental validation showed that the system was able to acquire and transmit ECG signals with 
stable operation under controlled testing conditions . The results demonstrate that the system is 
capable of acquiring, transmitting, and visualizing ECG signals with sufficient quality for basic 
rhythm observation and waveform morphology analysis. While the system cannot currently 
replace certified clinical E CG equipment, it provides meaningful cardiac signal visualization 
suitable for preliminary assessment, remote monitoring, and educational or research applications. 
It also lays down the foundation for a scalable, upgradeable, locally available, and affordable ECG 
device. The ability to stream and archive ECG sessions through a web interface further enhances 
system usability and aligns with current trends in IoT-based healthcare platforms. 
Despite these positive outcomes, the project scope also introduced limitations that define clear 
directions for future work. Validation relied primarily on an ECG simulator and a recorded signal 
rather than human subjects, and the multi-channel measurements represent conditioned projections 
derived from shared inputs rather than fully independent physiological limb leads.  
In summary, the system fulfills its primary objective as a functional proof-of-concept for portable, 
IoT-based ECG monitoring. It establishes a solid technical foundation upon which more clinically 
robust signal processing, expanded validation, and product -level refinements can be built, 
supporting future development toward a practical and accessible cardiac monitoring solution.
